# Demand Radar Delivery Playbook

This is the operating standard behind the public offer. The goal is not to sound like a data team with 15 years of scars. The goal is to deliver a cleaner, more useful market read than most clients have ever received.

## Product Promise

Demand Radar helps a high-value service business decide what to say, build, fix, or prioritize next.

It does not promise revenue. It should improve a decision that already has real money behind it: a procedure line, high-ticket project, sales territory, target account segment, or conversion path.

Every recommendation must answer:

- What did we observe?
- Why does it matter commercially?
- What should the client do next?
- What evidence supports that call?

If a recommendation cannot point back to visible market behavior, remove it.

## Client Fit

Best fit:

- Average customer value is high enough that one good opportunity matters.
- The client can act on page, offer, ad, sales, or positioning recommendations.
- The decision-maker wants a clear market read, not a pile of dashboards.
- The team is calm, direct, and willing to give creative room.

Bad fit:

- Tiny-ticket services where the economics cannot support the work.
- Clients who want guaranteed outcomes.
- Clients who only want a generic content calendar or ad account audit.
- Clients who need every recommendation to be consensus-approved before the work starts.

## Tier Standards

### Demand Snapshot

Use when the lane is not fully validated yet.

Minimum output:

- One market, one service/procedure/project/buyer segment.
- 8-12 visible demand signals.
- 5-8 competitor or alternative-positioning observations.
- 5-7 buyer objections or hesitation patterns.
- 3-5 priority recommendations.
- Evidence appendix with links, screenshots, excerpts, or source notes.

Delivery time: 7-10 business days.

### Radar Buildout

Use when the client is ready to change pages, ads, outreach, or sales enablement.

Minimum output:

- Everything from the Snapshot.
- Ranked demand lanes by commercial usefulness.
- Competitor angle map.
- Objection-to-response map.
- Page/ad/outreach angle set.
- 30-day execution brief.
- Optional wireframe notes or message hierarchy.

Delivery time: 2-3 weeks.

### Monthly Demand Radar

Use when the client has budget and capacity to act every month.

Minimum monthly output:

- Demand movement summary.
- Competitor movement summary.
- Updated buyer objections.
- 3-5 current priorities.
- One strategic call or async walkthrough.
- Action log: what changed, what stayed, what to watch.

Minimum term: 3 months.

### Embedded Intelligence

Use for multi-location, multi-market, or account-level work.

Minimum output:

- Recurring intelligence rhythm.
- Market or account comparison.
- Leadership-level priority brief.
- Sales, campaign, or location-specific recommendations.
- Clear decision log.

Minimum term: 6 months.

## Source Categories

Use multiple source categories. Do not make broad claims from one source type.

Search and demand:

- Search result pages.
- Related searches and autocomplete patterns.
- Keyword tools if available.
- Google Business Profile signals where relevant.
- Local/service modifiers.

Competitors:

- Service/procedure/product pages.
- Landing pages.
- Offer pages.
- Pricing language or financing cues.
- Proof language.
- Calls to action.

Buyer hesitation:

- Reviews.
- Reddit, forums, or public communities where appropriate.
- People Also Ask.
- FAQs.
- Sales objections provided by the client.
- Comments and public questions.

Commercial context:

- Average job/procedure/account value from client input.
- Capacity constraints.
- Service areas.
- Margin or contribution assumptions when shared.
- Sales cycle or consult process.

B2B account signals:

- Hiring patterns.
- Vendor pages.
- Tech stack clues where visible.
- Funding, expansion, compliance, or operations signals.
- Role changes and public announcements.

## Scoring Model

Score each opportunity from 1-5 on:

- Intent: how close the signal is to buying action.
- Frequency: how often the pattern appears across sources.
- Value: whether the service line or account type is commercially meaningful.
- Friction: whether a clear objection is blocking action.
- Actionability: whether the client can do something about it now.

Priority formula:

`priority = intent + frequency + value + friction + actionability`

Use the score to guide the recommendation order. Do not show the formula unless it helps the client trust the logic.

## Deliverable Structure

Use this order:

1. Executive read: the 5-7 things the client needs to know.
2. Market lane: what we inspected and why.
3. Demand signals: what buyers appear to be looking for.
4. Competitor angles: what alternatives are saying.
5. Objections: what slows the buyer down.
6. Opportunities: what looks worth changing.
7. Recommendations: what to do next.
8. Evidence appendix: links, screenshots, source notes, and excerpts.

Keep the main brief short. Put backup proof in the appendix.

## Quality Checks

Before delivery:

- Every recommendation has evidence.
- No invented metrics.
- No fake precision.
- No unsupported revenue projections.
- No private or sensitive data in the deliverable.
- No generic advice that could apply to any business.
- The top 3 recommendations are commercially useful, not merely interesting.
- The language sounds like a smart operator, not a pitch deck.

## How To Beat Larger Data Teams

Do not try to out-dashboard them.

Win on:

- Faster path from market signal to business decision.
- Cleaner writing.
- Better taste in what matters.
- Less noise.
- More useful recommendations.
- Stronger connection between buyer hesitation and what the client should change.

The client should finish the brief thinking: "This is exactly what has been happening in our market, and now I know what to do about it."
